What each one actually is
1 · Tracker continuity

Keeps a repeated study's trend line intact over time. When a client changes a question or a scale, a researcher-approved map translates old waves to new, so the trend line survives instead of being rebuilt by hand.

2 · Answer before the decision date

At kickoff we record what decision the study feeds and when it gets made. If the date lands mid-study, we send an early read of the winner — but only once the numbers are stable and a researcher signs off.

3 · MCP access

A read-only connection so stakeholders ask questions of their own study data from Claude, Slack or their internal AI. Approved, cited answers only. No new interface to log into.

4 · Delivery friction

The unbilled back-and-forth after a report ships: 2.8 revision rounds per study, five days sitting in client review. Cause-code it for a quarter before anyone builds workflow.

5 · Self-serve dashboards

The number one request by volume, from the segment that pays least and churns most, on the exact surface Glimmer undercuts us on.

6 · Open-end theming

AI that groups free-text answers into themes. Ruth's team already does this with ChatGPT and would not pay for it separately.

5 · The substantial bet

Tracker continuity

A tracker's questions change, the trend line breaks, and someone rebuilds it by hand. We build that link once and keep it.

What breaks today Marketing reworks the questionnaire. Old and new waves stop lining up.

The better part of a day, every wave — plus every time someone pulls a number out of an old deck and nobody can say whether it still compares.

What we build A question map, stored per tracker.

Old wording, new wording, and the transformation between them. Built once with a researcher, then applied to every wave that follows, and to any old number someone quotes.

Where the AI sits It proposes. It never decides.

The model matches questions across the two questionnaires, with the wording, a reason and a confidence score. A researcher approves each one; low confidence means not comparable. Every calculation runs in code.

How we know it works, before a client sees it

The evaluation set already exists: every trend deck a researcher has rebuilt by hand. We replay those waves, diff our answer against theirs, and count the times we would have said "comparable" when they said no. That number has to be zero.

The thin PRD, if you want the scope line
v1 scope

One tracker template, up to five historical waves. Comparison table and short narrative into the existing report flow, every number linked to its source question and transformation. Client sees lineage: source question, wave, transformation, reviewer.

Out of scope

Inventing bridges between questions that genuinely moved. Replacing researcher sign-off. Arbitrary cross-study Q&A. Dashboards.

Week one, before code: ten broken comparisons reviewed with researchers, interviews with the three largest tracker accounts, and one continuity read delivered entirely by hand to a design partner.

6 · The small bet

Answer before the decision, not after it.

Ruth's team delivered a clean study six days after the client had chosen. Good work, worth nothing — and it is the #2 ask across 410 logged requests.

What we add Three fields on the brief they already write.

What decision this study feeds, who makes it, and when that decision gets made. We already track a delivery deadline — that is how we know we hit it 71% of the time — but nothing tells us whether the deadline itself was already after the client decided.

No decision date, no product.

What we watch Whether the answer has settled.

Only on studies where the client is choosing between options and one has to win. At kickoff the researcher tells us which number decides it — we do not pick that.

Then, as responses come in, we watch one thing: is the same option still ahead, and is the gap big enough that the sample still to come could not close it?

What we send An early read a researcher has signed.

If the leader has held: the winner, the margin, two themes, the main risk, every number cited. Locked, versioned and marked directional, so the final report supersedes it.

If it is still moving, the answer is too early. Not a softer paragraph.

Where the work actually is

The three fields are a day of work. The gate is the build: a rule for when a lead is safe to call, backtested against last quarter's ~240 studies before a client sees anything, and nothing sends without a researcher accepting it. Small on purpose: 5–10 pilot studies to test a thesis, then it takes the substantial slot next quarter or it dies.

The other candidate for this slot: MCP
What it is

One read-only MCP server for one design partner. Stakeholders ask questions of their own study data from Claude, Slack or their internal LLM, instead of emailing Ruth's team. Approved, cited cuts only, scoped per account.

Why it is tempting

It answers the access demand without a dashboard war, gives sales something genuinely new to demo, and is the first thing we would ship that reaches brand managers in the tools they already use.

Why not both

The early read tests the timing thesis; MCP tests the access thesis. Capacity says pick one. I would decide after week-one discovery: if the ad-hoc volume traces back to the high payers, MCP takes this slot.

7 · Risk

What could go wrong, and what stops it.

Neither risk is "the model is inaccurate." Both are a client acting on something we got wrong.

1. We tell a client two waves are comparable when they are not.
What it costs
They show a CMO a trend line that is not real. We lose the account, and the reason they pay us: a person stood behind the number.
What stops it
A researcher approves every match. Anything the model is unsure about is marked not comparable and falls back to the manual rebuild.
Proof before launch
Replay last quarter's hand-rebuilt decks. Count the times we would have said "comparable" where the researcher said no. We ship at zero.
2. We send an early read that the finished study reverses.
What it costs
The client already acted on our number. A CMO stops trusting it — worse than being slow, which is the problem we set out to fix.
What stops it
We only send once the leader has stopped moving, a researcher signs every release, and the final report supersedes it.
Proof before launch
Test the rule against last quarter's ~240 studies: at every point we would have sent early, did it agree with the final one?
Either one, once, stops the rollout We go back to the manual path that week while we work out why.
When we would know we were wrong
Week one

Do two researchers agree on the same mapping for ten historically broken comparisons?

If they cannot agree, there is no ground truth to build against. Narrow to the easy cases, or stop.
Pilot · 5–10 studies

Zero unsafe matches, hours per wave falling, and early reads reaching the owner before the date.

Short of it, scope narrows. It does not get one more sprint.
The quarter

Extra waves and cuts bought by tracker accounts, against a matched baseline of accounts without the tool.

Not NRR. Nothing here moves 102 to 115 in 90 days and I would rather say so now.
And the third risk, the quiet one

Everyone likes the output and nobody buys more of it. That is why the quarter's test is extra waves and cuts from tracker accounts against a matched baseline, not a satisfaction score. Design partners who like it but show no expansion intent is a stop, not a nudge.

8 · Where to push me

The pushback I expect.

Nora is losing four deals a quarter. Why kill dashboards?

The lost deals are real, but the loudest demand comes from the 15%-of-revenue, 2×-churn slice, and Glimmer is 35% cheaper on exactly that surface — while its larger accounts come back to full service. The access need is real; the answer is querying, not a worse Tableau. That routes to MCP.

Decision timing is the better story. Why only the small bet?

Demand is not the problem: second only to dashboards across 410 logged requests, and slow turnaround is 30% of churn exits (n=17). The gap is ours. We hit the client-stated deadline 71% of the time, but the packet says plainly that we have no read on whether that deadline was already late against the real decision. So: are decision dates knowable at scoping, and can we call a winner early and be right? The pilot buys both.

Anya: 2.8 revision rounds is our margin walking out the door. Why is that only a diagnosis?

Because we do not know the cause. It is real money, but nothing tells us whether it is ambiguous briefs, missing cuts, or stakeholders who never saw the plan. Cause-code revisions for a quarter — an analyst afternoon a week, not a build. If one cause dominates, it tops next quarter's list with evidence.

Marcus: my two at-risk renewals are not feature problems. Nothing here saves them.

Agreed. A departed champion and a price consolidation do not get fixed by a roadmap. That is why the target is expansion inside accounts that already pay — GRR at 89% is a different problem with a different owner. What continuity does do is make us harder to consolidate away from.

The CEO: is this a defensible answer to "what is Northwind other than an agency with good taste"?

Anyone can produce an answer now — ChatGPT does the open-ends, Glimmer the dashboards. What is scarce is an answer someone has checked and will stand behind. "We deliver research faster" does not survive a follow-up. "Every number we give you has a name on it, and that person will defend it in front of your CMO" does, and the map keeps that judgement wave after wave.

And what I would push back on
Omar — the migration is 40% of Q3. What does it actually buy us, and could any of it slide?
Nora — Glimmer's larger accounts came back to full service. Which ones? Can we get in front of them?
Lena — two deals lost with our buyer not in the room. Did our champion get moved out, or replaced?
Anya — what actually gets revised? If it is the same thing every time, that is fixable without dev.

10 · How I built this

Three models, one argument, and a walk around the block.

01 · Interviews“You” interviewed me before you interviewed me.Claude researched the questions each interviewer would be likely to ask, and turned them into an interview prompt for ChatGPT voice. Then I talked to them on my evening walk around the block.
02 · Read firstEvery input, by hand, problems only.One Google Doc of everything that looked wrong in the data. No solutions allowed until the list was finished.
03 · The modelRan it through my thoughtful-execution model.A decision tree, not a wish list: evidence → problem area → opportunity → hypothesis → solution. Nothing reaches the roadmap without evidence behind it, and the options I killed stay on the map.
04 · Same brief, three waysClaude, Codex and Grok each wrote the memo.Independently, from the same packet. Predictably, they did not agree on what to build.
05 · The fightThen I made them argue about it.Put the three memos in front of each other in Grok and had them defend their picks — specifically on which option actually moves NRR. The disagreement was the useful part.
06 · This deckDesigned and branded by AI, from your own site.Colours, type and the brand motif pulled from the company’s own site. The judgement calls in it are mine.
